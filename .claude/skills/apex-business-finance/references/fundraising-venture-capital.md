# Fundraising & Venture Capital

## Scope
Raising institutional capital via venture rounds: investor expectations, term sheets, due diligence, and the cap table implications.

## Core principles
- Venture capital is not free money; it's high-risk capital that expects to own a meaningful percentage (15-25% per funding round) and exits (acquisition or IPO) within 7-10 years at 10x+ returns.
- Investors look for the "venture case": a market large enough ($1B+) and a team nimble enough to carve out a defensible position within 3-5 years; a profitable lifestyle business is a good business, not a venture business.
- The term sheet defines participation (preferred over common), liquidation preferences (how much they get in a down exit before common holders), and anti-dilution; liquidation preference math can make a 1x return look like loss to common shareholders in a down exit.
- Investor stage expectations: seed (~$500k-$2M) expects vision and market fit hypothesis, Series A (~$5-20M) expects traction (revenue, users, or usage), Series B+ expects unit economics and a clear path to profitability.
- Due diligence kills most deals: cap table cleanliness, option pool size, IP assignment (did your first engineer keep IP rights?), and customer concentration (is 30% of revenue one account?).

## Apex practices
- Maintain a clean cap table from day one: founder fully diluted ownership should assume 4-5 funding rounds and employee option pool; if you promise 20% options post-Series A, you're over-diluted.
- Build a 5-year financial model (P&L, balance sheet, cash flow) that shows a path to profitability or clear inflection point; "we'll figure it out" kills institutional investors.
- Pitch investors who have backed similar businesses; experienced investors in your space are more likely to add value and less likely to ghost if diligence stalls.
- Negotiate cap table items (option pools, anti-dilution treatment, board seats) as carefully as valuation; a high valuation with full-ratchet anti-dilution can be worse than a lower valuation with broad-based weighted average anti-dilution.

## Pitfalls
- Taking investment from an investor who can't reference successful exits in your space; they're learning at your expense.
- Raising before you have product-market fit or clear customer traction; "we have a great team" fundraising burns runway and dilutes heavily.
- Assuming participation rights are aligned; read the liquidation preference carefully — some structures mean Series A holders get paid before common shareholders in a down exit.
