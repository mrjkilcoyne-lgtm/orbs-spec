# Remote Work

## Scope
Making distributed and hybrid teams work: async communication, documentation culture, time zones, and remote-first practices.

## Core principles
- Remote work fails as "the office, but on Zoom" and succeeds as a different operating system: async-first, written-first, with synchronous time reserved for what genuinely needs it (conflict, bonding, high-bandwidth design).
- Default-to-documentation replaces ambient awareness: in an office, context spreads by osmosis; remote, anything not written down effectively didn't happen — the handbook-first model (GitLab) makes the written source authoritative, not the hallway.
- Time-zone spread is a budget: ~4 hours of overlap supports real-time collaboration; beyond that you're an async organization whether you admit it or not, and pretending otherwise burns whoever is at the edge of the map with 6am meetings.
- Hybrid's failure mode is the two-tier team: when some people share a room, decisions migrate to the room and remote members become second-class — "one remote, all remote" (everyone on their own camera, decisions in writing) is the known countermeasure.
- Remote turns loneliness and overwork into the top occupational risks (Buffer's State of Remote Work surveys, year after year): the boundary between work and life must be constructed deliberately because the commute no longer constructs it for you.

## Apex practices
- Write decisions and context in durable, searchable places (docs, issue threads) instead of chat; Slack is where information goes to die — treat it as ephemeral by policy.
- Over-communicate state proactively: daily written check-ins or visible work-in-progress replace the desk drive-by; silence in remote teams reads as either "fine" or "drowning" and managers can't tell which.
- Engineer serendipity on purpose: donut pairings, optional co-working calls, and periodic in-person offsites (the highest-ROI remote spend — relationship capital built in 3 days funds 6 months of async trust).
- Measure output and outcomes, never presence: green-dot monitoring and mouse-jiggler wars are the death spiral of remote trust; if you can't tell whether someone is performing without watching their status dot, the problem is your goal-setting.

## Pitfalls
- Meeting sprawl as a trust prosthetic: filling calendars with syncs to simulate oversight, which destroys the maker time that was remote work's main gift.
- Onboarding remotely with the in-office playbook: new hires without deliberate connection-building (assigned buddy, structured intro 1:1s, written 30/60/90) flounder invisibly for months.
- Letting the loudest time zone win: scheduling everything for HQ convenience until the far-zone engineers quietly disengage and then loudly resign.

## Tools & references
GitLab's public all-remote handbook (the canon), "Remote: Office Not Required" (Fried & Hansson), Buffer State of Remote Work reports, Matt Mullenweg's five levels of distributed autonomy.
