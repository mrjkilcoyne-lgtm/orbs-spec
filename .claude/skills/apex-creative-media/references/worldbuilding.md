# Worldbuilding

## Scope
Designing coherent fictional universes: geography, culture, history, rules, and consistency across narratives, games, and interactive worlds.

## Core principles
- A world is not a backdrop; it's a character with its own internal logic, constraints, and consequences — if magic exists, why doesn't it solve every problem? If technology is advanced, why are characters struggling with X?
- Consistency rules: establish boundaries early (what's possible, what's not) and respect them; breaking your own rules feels like cheating, and readers/players notice.
- Culture (customs, language, aesthetics) emerges from geography (climate, resources, isolation) and history (wars, alliances, migrations) — not the reverse; a desert culture doesn't develop the same way as a forest culture.
- Less is more: audiences fill in gaps with imagination; overexplaining a world makes it feel smaller and removes mystery.
- Every element must be loadbearing: if a detail doesn't affect plot, character, or atmosphere, it's ornament; ornament is fine, but it shouldn't disguise lack of substance.

## Apex practices
- Start with one constraint (magic system, technology level, political structure) and build consequences: if magic is rare, wars are different; if tech is advanced, labor is different.
- Create a timeline (even if not all revealed) so historical events have coherent causality; contradictions destroy immersion.
- Build a style/aesthetic identity (visual, linguistic, thematic) so the world feels distinctive; Terry Pratchett's Discworld and N.K. Jemisin's Broken Earth have distinct voices despite similar genre.
- Limit exposition: characters shouldn't explain the world to the reader; they live in it and take it for granted (show, don't tell applies to worldbuilding too).

## Pitfalls
- Overwhelming the story with worldbuilding detail; the world serves the story, not vice versa.
- Infodumping: characters explaining their world to each other in unnatural dialogue because you need the reader to know it.
- Ignoring power dynamics: a world needs tension, conflict, and asymmetry; utopias are narratively boring.

## Tools & references
Sanderson's Laws of Magic (establishing consistent magic systems), Tolkien's work and appendices (comprehensive worldbuilding), Le Guin's "The Lathe of Heaven" (exploring consequences), Watababe's "The Worldbuilder's Guidebook," collaborative worldbuilding (D&D, RPGs).
