# Arts and Crafts

## Scope
Morris's rebellion against industrial shoddiness: honesty of materials, unity of designer and maker, joy in labor — the ethics layer of design.

## Core principles
- "Have nothing in your houses that you do not know to be useful, or believe to be beautiful" — Morris's filter is a shipping criterion: every element justifies itself by use or beauty, ideally both.
- Truth to materials: wood should look like wood, stone like stone — and a webpage like a document, a button like software. Faking one material in another (skeuomorphic marble, fake hand-drawn fonts) is the movement's cardinal sin.
- The maker's hand is value, not defect: machine perfection is cheap; evidence of judgment and craft is what mass production cannot fake. In code: the well-considered API bears the same hand-of-the-maker quality.
- Designer and maker must not be severed: Morris learned weaving, dyeing, and calligraphy before designing for them. Design decisions made by people who can't build them produce ornamental nonsense — the argument for designers who code and engineers who design.
- Beauty belongs to everyone (and the tension is instructive): Morris wanted democratic beauty but handcraft priced out the people; resolving that tension — craft quality at scale — is the modern design system's whole project.

## Apex practices
- Learn the material's grain before styling it: what does this medium (terminal, e-ink, CSS, print) do easily, and what does it resist? Design with the grain.
- Pattern as structure: Morris's wallpapers are rigorous repeats with organic surface — build systems (tokens, grids, components) that read as living, not mechanical.
- Keep a "nothing merely decorative" pass: for each ornament, name its use or cut it — ornament that survives becomes identity.
- Sign the work: craft accountability (changelogs, colophons, named owners) raises quality the way the guild mark did.

## Pitfalls
- Nostalgia cosplay: hand-lettered rusticity applied as a style while the underlying product is careless — the movement was about how things are made, not how they look.
- Craft as excuse for cost/slowness with no user-visible quality difference.
- Rejecting tooling wholesale; Morris used the best presses of his day — the target is shoddiness, not machinery.

## Tools & references
Morris's "The Beauty of Life," Kelmscott Press books, Ruskin "The Nature of Gothic," Gustav Stickley's The Craftsman; contemporary echo: "The Nature of Order" (Alexander).
