# Contracts Basics

## Scope
Commercial contract fundamentals: what makes a contract enforceable, interpreting key terms, negotiating liability and indemnification, and avoiding ambiguity.

## Core principles
- A contract requires offer, acceptance, and consideration (something of value exchanged); a vague agreement with no clear terms is not enforceable — specificity matters.
- Liability is negotiated in four dimensions: total liability cap (how much either party can lose), consequential damages (lost profits excluded or included?), indemnification (who reimburses whom for third-party claims?), and insurance requirements (who holds errors & omissions insurance?).
- Force majeure and termination clauses define exit ramps; if a vendor goes out of business or a customer faces bankruptcy, who bears the cost? These clauses matter when they matter most (during crises).
- Ambiguity is interpreted against the drafter; if you write "we'll provide service on demand" and the other side interprets it as "all hours," you lose the dispute. Use precise language (SLA uptime percentages, response time windows, defined support hours).
- NDA and confidentiality clauses need specificity: what's confidential (code, customer lists, pricing), who can access it, for how long, and what's the remedy for breach (injunction, monetary damages)?

## Apex practices
- Use templates for repeatable contracts (customer contracts, employment agreements, vendor agreements) and update them quarterly; starting from scratch for every deal is slow and error-prone.
- Define success metrics in service contracts with specific SLAs (uptime 99.5%, incident response <4 hours); "best effort" or "reasonable efforts" is unenforceable.
- Negotiate liability caps early (many vendors will refuse 10x damages on a $10k deal); reasonable caps protect both parties and reduce disputes.
- Have a lawyer review contracts once (in template form), then non-lawyers can negotiate from the template; full legal review on every deal is expensive and slows progress.

## Pitfalls
- Signing contracts with undefined terms ("market rates," "reasonable support") that create disputes later; specificity is cheaper than litigation.
- Asymmetric risk (you waive liability, they don't) without compensation; check that risk allocation matches value exchange.
- Ignoring renewal and termination clauses; many contracts auto-renew and lock in bad terms if you don't manage renewal dates.
