# Mergers & Acquisitions Basics

## Scope
Buying and selling companies: deal structure, valuation approaches, due diligence, and integration planning.

## Core principles
- Deal value is split between purchase price (how much the buyer pays) and deal structure (when and how it's paid); an "earn-out" (payment based on post-acquisition performance) reduces upfront risk but can delay founder payoff.
- All-cash acquisitions are rare; most tech acquisitions are stock deals (buyer pays in stock), stock + cash (earn-out), or assumption of debt. Tax implications differ (qualified reorganization vs. taxable event).
- Due diligence (investigating the target company) reveals red flags: customer concentration (top customer is 40% revenue), tech debt (100k lines of spaghetti code), or legal issues (pending litigation).
- Purchase agreements include reps and warranties (seller certifies that described facts are true) and indemnification (buyer compensated for breaches); disputes often arise from disagreements on reps (is a customer "at risk" of leaving?).
- Integration is where 60% of deal value is lost or won; combining teams, systems, and processes requires active planning (who reports to whom, which systems migrate when, which people stay).

## Apex practices
- Hire experienced M&A counsel before offer stage; DIY legal work leads to disputes later that cost 10x what counsel would have cost upfront.
- Run a detailed customer reference check during diligence; call 5-10 of the top customers and ask about churn risk, contract strength, and perceived product direction.
- Negotiate reps and warranties carefully; a seller rep "no litigation pending" can expose the buyer to lawsuits the seller knew about — push back on time limits and materiality baskets.
- Build a 100-day integration plan pre-close (which systems will migrate first, who leads each workstream, what are the top risks?); post-close is too late.

## Pitfalls
- Overpaying (buyer disease — falling in love with the target); the best negotiators assume they might walk away.
- Underestimating due diligence scope; a 50-person acquirer diligencing a 200-person acquisition needs 20% of the acquirer's best people on the project, not a contractor.
- Assuming equity holders and employees will stay post-close; retention is rarely automatic — plan equity refresh or bonuses for key staff.
