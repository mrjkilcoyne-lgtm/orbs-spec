# Budgeting & Forecasting

## Scope
Planning future financial performance: revenue forecasts, expense budgets, cash flow projections, and scenario planning.

## Core principles
- Forecasts are always wrong; the goal is to be consistently wrong in a known direction (bias), not randomly wrong (noise). A model that overestimates revenue by 10% every quarter is adjustable; a model that swings ±30% is noise.
- Bottom-up vs. top-down forecasts serve different purposes: bottom-up (sum the sales pipeline, multiply by win rate) tests feasibility; top-down (market size × expected share) tests ambition. Both should converge or the forecast is suspect.
- Budgets are political agreements on spending authority; forecasts are predictions of what will happen. Conflating them creates perverse incentives (spend the budget to justify next year's, or, use-it-or-lose-it behavior).
- Variance analysis (actual vs. budget) should tie to specific causes (new hire, price increase, delay) not just "we were off"; causes are actionable, variance is not.
- Scenario planning (base case, bull case, bear case) is more useful than a single forecast; base case assumptions should be testable within a quarter or two, invalidating the forecast early enough to act.

## Apex practices
- Roll out a 13-week cash flow forecast (13-week rolling window, updated weekly); cash flow is more relevant than P&L for survival.
- Forecast revenue by segment (product, channel, geography, customer size) and grow them independently; aggregate forecasts hide deterioration (enterprise growing, mid-market shrinking).
- Build a salary expense forecast with headcount plan and timing of hires; many companies miss forecasts by ignoring the ramping cost of growing headcount.
- Create a scenario sensitivity model (what if churn is 1% worse? what if CAC is 20% higher?) and maintain a response plan (if scenario X triggers, we'll cut Y); reactive adjustments are slower and more painful.

## Pitfalls
- Forecasting revenue based on last quarter's trend without considering seasonality or channel mix shifts; retail has Q4 peaks, B2B has January budget flushes.
- Budgeting expenses as a percentage of revenue (R&D as 20% of revenue) instead of absolute headcount and projects; this creates pressure to cut when growth slows, defeating the purpose.
- Ignoring working capital changes in the forecast; revenue growth consumes cash (AR buildup, inventory) before it generates cash; forecasts that hit revenue targets but miss cash targets are useless.
