# Product Strategy

## Scope
Choosing where to play and how to win: the diagnosis, guiding policy, and coherent actions that connect company mission to what the product team builds.

## Core principles
- Strategy is diagnosis → guiding policy → coherent actions (Rumelt, "Good Strategy Bad Strategy"); a list of goals or a vision statement is not a strategy — it must explain what you will NOT do and why you'll win.
- Strategy lives at the intersection of durable customer problems and durable competitive advantage (network effects, switching costs, scale economies, counter-positioning — Hamilton Helmer's 7 Powers); features without a power are rented, not owned.
- The kernel question is "why us, why now": what change in technology, regulation, or behavior opened a window that incumbents can't or won't exploit (Clayton Christensen's disruption asymmetry — incumbents' margins make some responses irrational for them).
- Strategy cascades: company strategy constrains product strategy, which constrains roadmap, which constrains sprint scope; when a team can't explain how a feature ladders up, the cascade is broken, not the team.
- Good strategy concentrates force: pick one wedge segment and win it decisively before expanding ("bowling pin" — Geoffrey Moore's Crossing the Chasm), because being 10% better for everyone loses to being 10x better for someone.

## Apex practices
- Write the strategy as a one-to-two-page memo (diagnosis, policy, 3-5 coherent bets, explicit non-goals) and re-litigate it quarterly against evidence, not annually against politics.
- Stress-test with pre-mortems: "It's 18 months later and this strategy failed — why?" surfaces the assumptions doing the most load-bearing work.
- Map your position with Wardley mapping or 7 Powers before committing engineering-years; the map shows which components are commodity (buy) vs. differentiating (build).
- Instrument the strategy's leading indicators (wedge-segment win rate, sales cycle in the target vertical) so you can detect drift within a quarter, not after a year.

## Pitfalls
- "Strategy" documents that are really goal lists ("grow revenue 40%") with no diagnosis of why growth is currently blocked and no trade-offs.
- Copying a competitor's visible moves without their invisible economics — Costco's membership model or Amazon's scale don't transplant.
- Peanut-buttering resources across every segment and initiative because saying no is politically expensive; strategy that offends nobody beats nothing.

## Tools & references
Rumelt "Good Strategy Bad Strategy," Helmer "7 Powers," Moore "Crossing the Chasm," Wardley maps, Christensen "The Innovator's Dilemma," Gibson Biddle's DHM model.
