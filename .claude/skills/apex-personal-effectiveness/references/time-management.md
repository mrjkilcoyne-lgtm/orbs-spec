# Time Management

## Scope
Allocating finite hours across competing demands: planning systems, prioritization, calendars, and defending time from entropy.

## Core principles
- Parkinson's Law is real: work expands to fill the time allotted, so timeboxing (fixed slot, variable scope) beats open-ended task lists for anything estimable.
- Prioritization is triage, not ordering: the Eisenhower distinction (urgent vs. important) matters because urgent-but-unimportant work systematically crowds out important-but-non-urgent work unless you schedule the latter first.
- Your calendar is the ground truth, not your to-do list — a task without a time slot is a wish; time-blocking converts intentions into commitments and exposes overcommitment arithmetically.
- Planning fallacy (Kahneman & Tversky) means your estimates are systematically optimistic; use reference-class forecasting — ask "how long did similar tasks actually take?" — and pad by the historical ratio, typically 1.5–2x.
- Saying no is the highest-leverage skill: every yes is an implicit no to something else; evaluate requests against your top three priorities, not against whether you have a free slot.

## Apex practices
- Run a weekly review (GTD-style): empty all inboxes, reconcile calendar vs. priorities, choose 3 outcomes for the week — 30 minutes that prevents days of drift.
- Front-load the day's hardest cognitive work into your first 90-minute block before opening email or chat; defend it as a recurring calendar hold.
- Batch shallow work (email, admin, approvals) into 2 fixed daily windows instead of interleaving; context-switching costs are 10–20 minutes per switch (Mark, UC Irvine).
- Track actuals vs. estimates for two weeks with a simple time log; the calibration data kills the planning fallacy faster than any technique.

## Pitfalls
- Optimizing task throughput while never asking whether the tasks matter — efficient production of the wrong output.
- Zero slack: scheduling 100% of capacity guarantees cascading failure the moment anything overruns (queueing theory says keep utilization under ~80%).
- Tool-hopping: switching productivity apps quarterly as procrastination dressed up as meta-work.

## Tools & references
Allen's "Getting Things Done," Newport's "Deep Work," Kahneman's "Thinking, Fast and Slow" (planning fallacy), time-blocking in any calendar, Toggl/RescueTime for actuals.
