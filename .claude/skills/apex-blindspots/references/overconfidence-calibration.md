# Overconfidence & Calibration

## Scope
The gap between stated confidence and actual hit rate — in estimates, diagnoses, claims, and plans.

## Core principles
- Calibration is measurable: of the claims you make at "90% sure," ~90% should be true; most experts run overconfident by 15-30 points, and fluency makes it worse.
- Confidence should track evidence weight, not articulation quality: a well-worded guess is still a guess; eloquence is the most seductive miscalibration engine.
- The planning fallacy is overconfidence about time: inside-view estimates ignore the distribution of similar past projects (reference-class forecasting fixes this: what did tasks like this actually take?).
- Distinguish aleatory from epistemic uncertainty: "the dice haven't rolled yet" vs "I don't know the rules" — the second kind can be reduced by looking, so look before quoting odds.
- Precision signals must match knowledge: "about 40 minutes" when you know ±10, "somewhere between 20 minutes and 3 hours" when you don't — false precision is a lie with extra decimal places.

## Detection tests
- Can I name what evidence would change my mind? (No answer = conviction, not conclusion.)
- Have I checked this claim against a source/run/measurement, or does it just "feel known"?
- Is my estimate based on this task's steps (inside view) or on how long similar tasks took (outside view)?

## Countermeasures
- Attach explicit confidence markers to nontrivial claims and track your hit rate where feedback exists.
- Give ranges with the honest width; widen until you'd bet at the stated odds.
- Before finalizing an estimate, find three comparable past cases and start from their median.

## Tools & references
"Superforecasting" (Tetlock), calibration training quizzes, reference-class forecasting (Kahneman/Flyvbjerg), Brier scores.
