# Mental Models

## Scope
A cross-disciplinary toolkit of compressed causal patterns — from economics, biology, physics, math — applied to everyday reasoning and decisions.

## Core principles
- Munger's latticework thesis: you need models from multiple disciplines because "to the man with a hammer, everything looks like a nail" — single-model thinkers force-fit their one lens onto every problem.
- Second-order thinking separates good from plausible decisions: "and then what?" — rent control lowers rents (first order) and shrinks housing supply (second order); most policy and product failures are unexamined second orders.
- Inversion (Jacobi via Munger): many problems are easier solved backward — instead of "how do I succeed?", ask "what would guarantee failure?" and avoid it; avoiding stupidity is more tractable than seeking brilliance.
- The map is not the territory (Korzybski): every model omits detail by design; the failure mode is not using models but forgetting their boundary conditions — GDP isn't welfare, IQ isn't wisdom, the org chart isn't the org.
- Incentives are the strongest force in systems ("show me the incentive and I'll show you the outcome"): before attributing behavior to character or culture, audit what the actors are actually rewarded for.

## Apex practices
- Build a personal checklist of ~20 workhorse models — opportunity cost, compounding, margin of safety, feedback loops, base rates, Chesterton's Fence, principal-agent problems, selection effects — and run decisions through it explicitly.
- Apply Chesterton's Fence before removing anything: don't take down a fence until you know why it was put up; "this looks pointless" is a claim about your knowledge, not the fence.
- Hunt for selection effects first in any surprising statistic — survivorship bias (Wald's armored bombers), sampling on the dependent variable — they explain more anomalies than any causal story.
- Use compounding arithmetic on habits and debts alike: 1% better daily ≈ 37x/year, but the same math runs in reverse for technical debt, deferred maintenance, and trust.

## Pitfalls
- Model name-dropping as analysis: citing "first principles" or "antifragility" without doing the actual decomposition or asymmetry math.
- Applying models outside their domain — Nash equilibria to your marriage, evolutionary just-so stories to every behavior.
- Collecting models without deploying them: a latticework you never consult is trivia; the checklist must be attached to real decisions.

## Tools & references
Munger's "Poor Charlie's Almanack," Farnam Street's "The Great Mental Models" series, Meadows' "Thinking in Systems," Dörner's "The Logic of Failure."
