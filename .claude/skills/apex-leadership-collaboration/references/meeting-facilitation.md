# Meeting Facilitation

## Scope
Designing and running meetings that produce decisions and alignment — and killing the ones that don't.

## Core principles
- Every meeting has exactly one legitimate type — decide, create, align, or bond — and mixing types is why meetings fail: a decision meeting hijacked by brainstorming decides nothing; know the type before sending the invite.
- The facilitator's product is the group's thinking, not their own: your job is airtime distribution, conflict surfacing, and convergence — the moment you're arguing your position, you've abandoned the role (in high-stakes meetings, split facilitator and stakeholder into two humans).
- Meetings cost headcount-hours and compound: a weekly 60-minute meeting with 8 people is ~50 person-days per year — price it like a hire, and default every invite to optional with notes published.
- Divergence before convergence, and never both at once: generate options silently in writing first (brainwriting beats brainstorming — Diehl & Stroebe showed groups shouting ideas produce fewer, due to production blocking and anchoring), then evaluate.
- No agenda with a desired outcome, no meeting: "discuss X" is not an outcome; "leave with a decision on X and an owner" is.

## Apex practices
- Open with the decision to be made and who makes it (consent, vote, or named decider) — ambiguity about the decision rule is why groups "decide" the same thing three meetings running.
- Use round-robins and silent writing to break the seniority-speaks-first pattern; the highest-paid person's opinion (HiPPO) anchoring first is the single biggest information destroyer in group settings.
- End with 5 minutes of explicit capture: decisions made, actions with owners and dates, and what's deliberately deferred — then publish within the hour.
- Audit recurring meetings quarterly with attendees: keep, shrink, merge, or kill; every recurring meeting should re-justify its existence or die.

## Pitfalls
- The status meeting that could be a dashboard: synchronous time spent reading information at each other that async text would deliver better.
- Letting "consensus" mean "we talked until the dissenters got tired" — decide the decision rule up front, or exhaustion becomes your governance model.
- Inviting for politeness: every marginal attendee raises coordination cost and lowers candor; send notes to the curious, invitations to the necessary.

## Tools & references
"Death by Meeting" (Lencioni), "The Surprising Science of Meetings" (Rogelberg), Amazon silent-start memos, Liberating Structures (1-2-4-All, etc.), Diehl & Stroebe (1987) on brainstorming.
