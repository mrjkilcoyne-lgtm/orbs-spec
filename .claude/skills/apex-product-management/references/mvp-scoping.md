# MVP Scoping

## Scope
Cutting the smallest thing that tests the riskiest assumption: MVP definitions, riskiest-assumption tests, and scope negotiation.

## Core principles
- An MVP is an experiment, not a small product (Ries, "The Lean Startup"): its output is validated learning about the riskiest assumption, and "minimum" is defined by what that test requires — nothing else earns a slot.
- Order risks explicitly: desirability (do they want it?) usually dominates feasibility (can we build it?) for new products; teams default to building because feasibility risk is the one engineers can retire at their desk.
- Cheaper tests before expensive ones: landing-page smoke test → concierge (do it manually) → Wizard of Oz (fake the backend) → single-feature build; Zappos tested demand by photographing shoes in stores and buying retail per order — zero inventory systems.
- Cut scope by depth of polish and breadth of cases, never by breaking the core loop: an MVP that can't complete its one job end-to-end tests nothing (the skateboard→bike→car sequence, not a car chassis with no wheels — Kniberg's sliced-value picture).
- Define the kill/persevere criteria before launch: "if <20% of trial users complete the flow twice in week one, we stop" — an MVP without pre-committed thresholds becomes a permanent v1 by default.

## Apex practices
- Write the riskiest assumption as a falsifiable sentence ("SMB owners will connect their bank account during first session") and design the smallest artifact that could prove it false.
- Timebox MVPs in weeks, not quarters; if the "minimum" needs six months, re-negotiate the assumption being tested, not the team's velocity.
- Run concierge versions with 5-10 design partners paying something (money, data, public reference) — payment converts polite interest into real evidence.
- Plan the second release before shipping the first: an MVP with no follow-through burns early adopters and poisons the well for the real launch.

## Pitfalls
- "MVP" as a euphemism for a bad v1 of the full vision — shipped broadly, judged as a product, generating churn instead of learning.
- Testing with a Wizard-of-Oz that can't scale and never scheduling the "now automate it" reckoning until unit economics collapse.
- Scope creep via "it just needs" accretion: every stakeholder adds one small must-have and the experiment quietly becomes a two-quarter build.
