# Reading Strategies

## Scope
Reading for comprehension, retention, and use: levels of reading, syntopical reading, note-making, and matching technique to purpose.

## Core principles
- Reading speed and comprehension trade off mechanically: RSVP and "speed reading" past ~500 wpm gut comprehension (Rayner's eye-movement research); real gains come from deciding what not to read and reading the rest at the speed of thought.
- Adler's four levels (elementary, inspectional, analytical, syntopical) imply the key skill is switching levels deliberately: inspect everything (10 minutes: TOC, intro, conclusions, skim), then analytically read only what earned it.
- Comprehension is built on prior knowledge: reading about baseball is easy for baseball fans regardless of "reading skill" (Recht & Leslie); when a text is hard, the deficit is usually background, so pre-load context (survey, glossary, an easier overview first).
- Retention requires elaboration, not exposure: notes in your own words connected to what you know (elaborative encoding) outlast highlights by an order of magnitude — highlighting is among the weakest techniques in Dunlosky's review.
- A book is a conversation, not a sentence: syntopical reading — multiple books on one question, mapped against each other — produces understanding no single text contains, and is the working method of every serious researcher.

## Apex practices
- Interrogate before and during: convert headings to questions (SQ3R's survey-question move), and read to answer them — questions turn passive decoding into active retrieval.
- Write a "conversation with the author" margin/note layer: objections, connections, examples — then a 5-sentence summary from memory after each chapter; the summary attempt is the retention event.
- Route notes into a permanent system: literature notes → your-own-words permanent notes linked by theme (Ahrens/Zettelkasten) — the note you can find and connect in two years is the only note that mattered.
- Quit ruthlessly and triage: Keeper's rule of thumb — the abandon threshold should rise as you age (100-minus-your-age pages); every mediocre book finished is a great book unread.

## Pitfalls
- Completionism: reading every book linearly at one speed, giving the appendix the same attention as the thesis.
- Collector's fallacy: hoarding highlights, PDFs, and "read later" queues as if saving were reading.
- Reading only the easy and agreeable: comprehension grows on texts slightly beyond you (Adler's "reading above your level"), and judgment grows on texts you dispute.

## Tools & references
Adler & Van Doren's "How to Read a Book," Ahrens' "How to Take Smart Notes," SQ3R method, Dunlosky et al. (2013), Readwise/Zotero for capture, Zettelkasten note systems.
