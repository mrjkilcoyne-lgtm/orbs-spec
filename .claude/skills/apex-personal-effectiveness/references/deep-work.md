# Deep Work

## Scope
Producing high-value cognitive output through long, distraction-free concentration blocks, and structuring life to make them routine.

## Core principles
- Attention residue (Leroy, 2009): after a task switch, part of your attention stays on the prior task for 10–25 minutes; a "quick check" of Slack taxes the next half hour of thinking.
- Deep work capacity is trainable but bounded — even elites sustain roughly 4 hours/day of true deep work (Ericsson's violinist studies); scheduling 8 is self-deception.
- Depth requires a ritual with fixed location, duration, and rules (phone in another room, specific start cue) because willpower is a depletable trigger, not a sustainable mechanism.
- The value of deep work follows from the economics of scarcity: shallow work is easily replicable; the ability to master hard things quickly and produce at an elite level is not (Newport's core thesis).
- Boredom tolerance is the substrate: a mind trained to reach for the phone at every idle moment cannot sustain concentration; the training happens outside work hours too.

## Apex practices
- Choose a depth philosophy deliberately — monastic (near-total isolation), bimodal (deep days/weeks), rhythmic (same 2-hour block daily), or journalistic (opportunistic) — rhythmic wins for most people with meetings.
- Set a shutdown ritual with an explicit phrase and open-loop capture (write down every unresolved item and next step); the Zeigarnik effect keeps unclosed loops intruding otherwise.
- Measure lead metrics, not lag: track hours of deep work on a visible scoreboard (Newport's 4DX adaptation); output follows the tracked input.
- Use "productive meditation" — assign a single professional problem to a walk or commute and repeatedly re-center on it when the mind wanders.

## Pitfalls
- Treating open-plan-office availability and instant reply as professional virtue — busyness as a proxy for productivity.
- Scheduling deep blocks but leaving notifications on; a single interruption resets the entire ramp-up cost.
- Confusing deliberate practice with flow — improvement requires strain at the edge of ability, which often feels bad, not flowy.

## Tools & references
Newport's "Deep Work" and "Digital Minimalism," Leroy's attention-residue paper, Ericsson's "Peak," Freedom/Cold Turkey blockers, calendar time-blocking.
