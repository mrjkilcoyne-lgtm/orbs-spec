# Proposal & Grant Writing

## Scope
Persuasive writing for funding: establishing need, demonstrating capability, and structuring for review criteria.

## Core principles
- Proposals compete for limited resources; reviewers scan dozens. Your opening must signal relevance and urgency within 30 seconds.
- Credibility is half the battle: citing prior work, naming team expertise, and showing you understand the funder's priorities matter as much as the idea.
- Specificity wins over ambition: a narrowly scoped, well-supported proposal beats a grand vision with hand-waving. Reviewers ask "can they actually do this?"
- Gap framing: establish what's known, explicitly name the gap or problem, position your work as filling it. This narrative arc is irresistible.
- Budget alignment: every dollar should connect to a specific deliverable or outcome. Padding is obvious and kills trust; under-budgeting signals naiveté.

## Apex practices
- Study successful proposals in your field; reverse-engineer structure, tone, and evidence standards. One example is more valuable than generic guidance.
- Use scoring rubrics (often published by funders) as a checklist; map each section to scoring criteria so you hit every weighted dimension.
- Have non-expert reviewers read your proposal; if they don't grasp the problem and solution in 5 minutes, it's not clear enough.
- Manage red flags preemptively: if your team is new to the area, say so and mitigate (pilot data, advisory board, partnerships). Silence invites skepticism.

## Pitfalls
- Overselling without proof; reviewers have seen this and discount claims without evidence.
- Ignoring funder priorities; a great proposal misaligned with funding goals scores low. Read RFPs carefully.
- Inflating timelines or minimizing costs; reviewers have domain experience and will catch unrealistic estimates.

## Tools & references
Federal grant writing (NIH SMART criteria, NSF proposer's guide), private foundation guidelines, past winning proposals from your institution.
