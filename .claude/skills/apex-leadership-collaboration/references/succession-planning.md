# Succession Planning

## Scope
Identifying and developing future leaders, reducing key-person dependencies, and preparing the organization for leadership transitions.

## Core principles
- Succession planning is a leadership pipeline, not a backup; it should surface talent and accelerate growth even if the top role never opens.
- The best development happens on the job, not in training; move people into stretching assignments with guardrails and close coaching.
- Visibility of succession plans paradoxically reduces turnover—high performers know there's a path, slower trackers aren't stuck behind blocked promotion ladders.
- Transitions fail when the outgoing leader is checked out or the incoming leader isn't ready; overlap periods matter (typically 3-6 months co-leadership).
- A leader's legacy includes what they built that survives them; if the team falls apart on their exit, the foundation wasn't solid.

## Apex practices
- Maintain a simple talent matrix: for each key role (top 3-5), name the primary candidate and a backup (internal or development plan).
- Assign "inverse mentoring" where the potential successor takes on visible high-stakes work with the current leader watching and coaching (not shadowing passively).
- Create transition playbooks for major roles: documentation of key relationships, decision history, budget authority, vendor contracts—not "stuff in a wiki" but a live handoff.
- Plan exits proactively with 6-12 months notice; a rushed succession is worse than leaving the role open while you develop the replacement.

## Pitfalls
- Keeping succession plans secret from the high performers (fear they'll leave); this is backward—they'll leave anyway if they don't see a future.
- Promoting someone based on technical skill alone without assessing coaching ability, delegation, or whether they thrive under scrutiny (different from IC work).
- Abrupt exits that leave no ramp: a key person quits or is pushed out and nothing was documented; the team is now in crisis mode.
