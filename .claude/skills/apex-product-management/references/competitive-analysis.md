# Competitive Analysis

## Scope
Understanding the competitive field well enough to make positioning and roadmap decisions: intelligence gathering, structural analysis, and avoiding competitor-driven whiplash.

## Core principles
- Your real competitor is whatever the customer would do instead — including spreadsheets, interns, and doing nothing; JTBD framing ("what would they fire to hire you?") catches the non-obvious substitutes that win 70% of "no decision" deals.
- Analyze structure, not just features: Porter's Five Forces and 7 Powers explain WHY a competitor can sustain low prices or fast shipping (their cost structure, distribution lock-in, network effects); a feature-comparison spreadsheet explains nothing about what they'll do next.
- Compete on asymmetry: find the move that is rational for you and irrational for them (counter-positioning — e.g., a pure-SaaS attacking a license-revenue incumbent that can't cannibalize itself); matching an incumbent feature-for-feature plays their game with their budget.
- Win/loss data outranks feature matrices: 20 structured win/loss interviews per quarter tell you why deals actually close or die — usually positioning, trust, and workflow fit, not the feature gap sales cites.
- Watch trajectories, not snapshots: hiring pages, pricing changes, API deprecations, and changelog cadence reveal a competitor's strategy 2-3 quarters before their launch post does.

## Apex practices
- Maintain living battlecards per competitor (their pitch, where you win, where you lose, landmine questions to plant, proof points) and update them from every win/loss interview — stale battlecards are worse than none.
- Do the teardown work: run trials of competitor products quarterly, capture their onboarding, and map their pricing/packaging so claims come from usage, not their marketing site.
- Track a small watchlist of leading indicators per competitor (funding, key hires, partnership announcements, G2 review themes) in a monthly digest, not ad-hoc panic threads.
- Present competitive intel with an explicit "so what": each finding tied to a decision it should change (positioning tweak, roadmap acceleration, sales play) — intel without a decision attached is trivia.

## Pitfalls
- Roadmap whiplash: reprioritizing the quarter every time a competitor ships, guaranteeing you're permanently 6 months behind their strategy instead of ahead on your own.
- Feature-parity checklists as strategy — customers buy outcomes and workflow fit; parity spreadsheets systematically overweight what's visible and countable.
- Only studying named competitors while an adjacent product or a platform's bundled feature absorbs the job (the spreadsheet/notion/excel problem).

## Tools & references
Porter "Competitive Strategy" (Five Forces), Helmer "7 Powers," win/loss practice (Clozd, "Win/Loss Analysis" by Ellen Naylor), Klue/Crayon for battlecards, April Dunford "Obviously Awesome" for positioning implications.
