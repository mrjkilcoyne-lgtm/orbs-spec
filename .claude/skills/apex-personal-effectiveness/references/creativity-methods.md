# Creativity Methods

## Scope
Deliberate techniques for generating and developing novel, useful ideas: divergent/convergent phases, constraints, combination, and incubation.

## Core principles
- Creativity is combinatorial: new ideas are mostly remote associations between existing elements (Koestler's "bisociation," Mednick's remote-associates research) — the raw material is breadth of input, and the mechanism is forced or noticed connection.
- Divergence and convergence must be separated in time: judging while generating kills both (Osborn's original brainstorming insight); generate to a quota first, evaluate ruthlessly second — mixing modes is the most common process failure.
- Quantity drives quality: the equal-odds rule (Simonton's studies of scientists and composers) — creators' hit rates are roughly constant, so more output means more hits; Pauling's "the best way to have a good idea is to have a lot of ideas" is empirically right.
- Constraints enhance creativity rather than limit it: bounded problems ("write a story in six words," Dr. Seuss's 50-word "Green Eggs and Ham" bet) outperform blank pages because constraints prune the search space and force remote associations.
- Incubation is a real effect: stepping away after loading the problem improves solutions (meta-analysis: Sio & Ormerod, 2009), especially with low-demand activity like walking — Wallas's preparation→incubation→illumination→verification stages hold up.

## Apex practices
- Brainwrite instead of brainstorm: silent individual generation before group sharing (6-3-5 method) — nominal groups reliably outproduce interacting groups (Diehl & Stroebe's production-blocking studies).
- Use structured provocation when stuck: SCAMPER (substitute/combine/adapt/modify/put-to-other-use/eliminate/reverse), random-word forcing, or Eno's Oblique Strategies — the point is to displace the search, not to be right.
- Keep an idea ledger and collide it: capture fragments daily, review monthly, and deliberately pair unrelated entries — most "sudden" insights are slow hunches finally meeting their missing half (Johnson's "Where Good Ideas Come From").
- Prototype at the lowest possible fidelity early: sketch, paper, improv — evaluation of a rough artifact beats evaluation of a description, and cheap artifacts keep ego investment low enough to kill weak ideas.

## Pitfalls
- Waiting for inspiration: professionals schedule creation ("inspiration is for amateurs — I just get to work," Chuck Close); the muse attends the working.
- First-idea fixation: the first plausible concept anchors all subsequent thought (design fixation); force 3+ genuinely different framings before developing any.
- Group brainstorming as the default: unstructured groups produce fewer, worse, and more conformist ideas than the same people working alone then pooling.

## Tools & references
