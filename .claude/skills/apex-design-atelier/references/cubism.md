# Cubism

## Scope
Picasso/Braque's decomposition of the single viewpoint: analytic faceting, synthetic collage, simultaneity — as a working method for design and interfaces.

## Core principles
- The core move: show multiple truths at once. A guitar seen from five angles simultaneously is more truthful than one photographic angle — the same argument as a dashboard showing five projections of one dataset.
- Analytic phase (shatter): decompose the subject into facets on a shallow picture plane, muted palette so structure reads over color.
- Synthetic phase (rebuild): reassemble from found fragments — newspaper, chair caning, wallpaper. Collage legitimized "real material inside the image": the ancestor of embeds, screenshots-in-UI, mixed media.
- Shallow space, no window: Cubism killed the picture-as-window; the surface admits it's a surface. Flat design's honest pixels descend from this.
- Passage (Cézanne's gift): planes bleed into neighbors, edges open — facets connect into a continuous field rather than a shattered mirror.

## Apex practices
- Design brief use: when one view can't carry the truth (complex data, multi-stakeholder story), facet it — simultaneous partial views on a shared shallow plane, unified by restrained palette.
- Practice the two-phase discipline: analyze (fragment the subject into its aspects) before synthesizing (compose the fragments with hierarchy restored).
- Let materials stay themselves in collage: a screenshot should look like a screenshot; disguising fragments forfeits Cubism's honesty.
- Keep color subordinate when structure is the message — the analytic browns/greys were a deliberate volume knob.

## Pitfalls
- Fragmenting decoratively: facets that don't correspond to real aspects of the subject are noise cosplaying as analysis.
- Deep-space perspective sneaking back in and breaking the plane logic.
- Simultaneity without hierarchy — five equal views and no reading order.

## Tools & references
"Les Demoiselles d'Avignon," Braque's "Violin and Candlestick," "Still Life with Chair Caning," John Golding's "Cubism," MoMA's Cubism collection notes.
