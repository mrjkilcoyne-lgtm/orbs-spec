# Financial Statements

## Scope
The three core statements (income statement, balance sheet, cash flow statement) and how they interlock to tell the story of a company's health.

## Core principles
- The income statement (P&L) shows profit or loss over a period and is backward-looking; the balance sheet is a snapshot of assets, liabilities, and equity at a point in time; the cash flow statement reconciles them by showing where cash actually moved.
- A profitable company can be cash-negative (heavy capex, customer deposits outstanding, inventory buildup); a cash-positive company with losses is burning equity (typical venture playbook, also possible fraud).
- The balance sheet must balance (Assets = Liabilities + Equity) by definition; if it doesn't, the GL is broken, not the formula.
- Depreciation and amortization are non-cash expenses that reduce taxable income; a fast-growing SaaS company with $1M in cloud infrastructure capex this year shows $50k annual depreciation for the next 20 years (P&L burden, not cash burden).
- Revenue is recognized by policy (point of sale, installation, contract value spread, completion); cash is recognized on receipt; the gap (accounts receivable) is one of the first red flags in financial health (growing faster than revenue = collectibility issues).

## Apex practices
- Always reconcile the three statements: cash flow should equal income statement net income adjusted for depreciation and working capital changes, plus capex, plus debt/equity changes.
- Use a waterfall or bridge method to explain year-over-year changes in key line items; "revenue grew 30%" is less useful than "existing customers grew 40%, churn was -15%, new customers added 5%."
- Build a 3-statement model (linked P&L, balance sheet, cash flow) as your baseline forecast; this forces you to think through capex, working capital, and financing, not just revenue assumptions.
- Build the trailing twelve-month (TTM) P&L and balance sheet alongside quarterly/annual statements; TTM removes seasonality and shows underlying run-rate.

## Pitfalls
- Confusing net income (bottom line of P&L) with operating income; interest, taxes, and one-time items distort the operational picture.
- Failing to adjust for one-time revenue or unusual items; a company that took a one-time $10M grant that inflated the top line is less healthy than the P&L suggests.
- Ignoring working capital deterioration (AR growing faster than revenue, or inventory bloat); the company can be profitable on paper and out of cash.
