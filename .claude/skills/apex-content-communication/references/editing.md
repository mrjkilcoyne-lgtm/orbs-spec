# Editing

## Scope
Refining written content for clarity, consistency, flow, and voice; line editing, structural editing, and copyediting.

## Core principles
- Editing has levels: developmental (big-picture structure and content), line (sentence-level flow and clarity), and copy (grammar, spelling, consistency). Each requires different lenses.
- Every word should justify itself; deletion is often improvement. Aim for 20% cut in first pass.
- Read aloud to catch awkward phrasing, run-on sentences, and repetition that silent reading misses.
- Consistency (voice, terminology, formatting) is invisible when present, glaring when absent. Maintain a style guide and checklist.
- The best editors are invisible; they make writers better without imposing style. Suggestions beat rewrites.

## Apex practices
- Use a checklist: voice consistency, active voice, jargon elimination, paragraph length, readability metrics (Flesch-Kincaid).
- Work in multiple passes: first for structure, second for clarity, third for mechanics. Each pass has a focus.
- Ask "why?" for every phrase; if you can't explain it, the reader won't understand it.
- Use track changes or comments rather than silent rewrites; authors learn why edits matter.

## Pitfalls
- Over-editing to match personal style rather than serving the piece's voice; respect the author's voice.
- Editing for perfection at the cost of velocity; "good enough" shipped beats "perfect" in draft.
- Ignoring the audience; edit for readers, not grammar textbooks. Formal prose for academic papers, conversational for web content.

## Tools & references
Strunk-White "Elements of Style," Lanham's "Revising Prose" (sentence-level clarity), AP Stylebook and Chicago Manual of Style, Hemingway Editor (tool), developmental editing frameworks.
