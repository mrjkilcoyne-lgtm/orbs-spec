# Cross-Functional Collaboration

## Scope
Working effectively across engineering, product, design, data, sales, support, and other functions with different incentives, vocabularies, and rhythms.

## Core principles
- Each function optimizes a different objective by design — sales optimizes this quarter's revenue, engineering optimizes long-term change-cost, design optimizes user experience — so cross-functional friction is not dysfunction, it's the org working as intended; alignment happens at the shared outcome, not at the method.
- Vocabulary is the first integration layer: "done," "launch," "MVP," and "spec" mean materially different things per function, and half of cross-functional conflict is two functions confidently using the same word for different objects — build a glossary before building the roadmap.
- The empowered trio model (product manager, design lead, engineering lead deciding together, per Cagan) beats sequential handoffs: waterfall-with-functions ("PM writes spec → design draws it → eng builds it") produces feature factories where each function inspects the others' output instead of sharing the problem.
- Trust across functions is built transactionally before it's built relationally: deliver the small thing you promised, on the date you said, three times, and you've earned the benefit of the doubt that makes the big collaboration possible.
- Escalation is a feature, not a betrayal: two functions deadlocked at the working level should escalate jointly and fast ("we disagree, here are both views, we need a call by Friday") — slow-simmering deadlock costs more than any decision either way.

## Apex practices
- Co-locate around outcomes, not artifacts: embed the engineer in discovery calls and the PM in incident reviews — shared exposure to raw reality beats any handoff document.
- Make the other function's constraints visible: engineers who've seen the sales pipeline understand the deadline; PMs who've sat an on-call rotation understand the platform ask — trade ride-alongs.
- Write one-page "working with us" interface docs per team (what we own, how to ask, our SLAs, our planning rhythm) — treating team boundaries as APIs cuts coordination meetings dramatically.
- Establish a single shared success metric per initiative that every function's leadership signed; when incentives collide mid-project, you argue from the shared metric instead of re-fighting the org chart.

## Pitfalls
- Contract negotiation posture: functions communicating through requirement documents and blame-shielding email chains — by the time it's "per my last message," collaboration is already dead.
- The engineering-as-vendor trap: eng "takes requirements" without pushback and delivers exactly what was asked, which is reliably not what was needed — no function should be a ticket queue for another.
- Assuming malice where incentive explains: "sales promised an impossible feature" is sales doing its comped job; fix the commitment process (deal-desk review with eng sign-off), not the salesperson.

## Tools & references
Cagan "Inspired" and "Empowered" (product trios), Team Topologies interaction modes, Lencioni "Silos, Politics and Turf Wars," RACI/DACI charters for shared initiatives.
