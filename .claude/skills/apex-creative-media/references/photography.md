# Photography

## Scope
Capturing and composing light to tell stories: exposure, composition, light direction, lens choice, and understanding the camera-subject-viewer relationship.

## Core principles
- Exposure (aperture, shutter speed, ISO) is a triangle of tradeoffs: aperture controls depth of field (subject isolation vs. environmental context), shutter speed freezes or blurs motion, ISO trades image grain for low-light capability — each choice is conceptual, not technical.
- Composition is not rules; it's proximity and priority: what's in frame, what's left out, and where your eye is drawn first decides the story. Foreground-subject-background layering creates depth on a 2D surface.
- Light direction (key, fill, backlight) determines mood and form: sidelighting reveals texture, backlighting separates subject from background, overhead flattens — the position of the light source is as important as its quality.
- A compelling image has clear hierarchy: one point of focus, supporting elements, and elimination of distractions. Amateurs add; professionals subtract.
- The camera is a tool that captures what's there; the photographer's job is to see what will be compelling, position themselves in the right place at the right time, and understand how the sensor transforms light into pixels.

## Apex practices
- Shoot the same subject from 3+ angles and distances before accepting a frame — your first instinct is rarely your best, and iteration teaches composition faster than theory.
- Use the "rule of thirds" as a starting point, then deliberately break it once you understand what it's doing (balancing elements, avoiding dead center).
- Understand your camera's metering and autofocus modes deeply, then learn to override them — the camera's choices often don't match your intention.
- Study photographs you admire and articulate why (color, composition, timing, subject, story) — reverse-engineering great work teaches faster than tutorials.

## Pitfalls
- Believing the gear matters more than seeing; a skilled photographer makes a phone camera sing, while an amateur wastes expensive gear.
- Over-processing or over-editing; the best photographs look natural or have a cohesive, intentional aesthetic, not HDR-blasted or filtered.
- Centering the subject always, ignoring context — environmental photography tells deeper stories.
