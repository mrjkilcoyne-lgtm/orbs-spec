# Note-Taking Systems

## Scope
Personal knowledge capture: structure, linking, and retrieval for long-term learning and decision-making.

## Core principles
- Capture is cheap; retrieval is expensive. Notes optimized for easy capture (brain-dump style) are hard to find later. Structure at capture time pays dividends.
- Linking (connecting related ideas) is more powerful than tagging or filing. A linked graph lets ideas remix and compound.
- Fleeting notes (temporary capture) vs. literature notes (what a book/article says) vs. permanent notes (your synthesis) are different artifacts with different purposes; conflating them creates bloat.
- Review cadence matters: daily (incubate ideas), weekly (consolidate), monthly (connect threads). Notes ignored for 6 months are digital dust.
- Serendipity (discovering relevant old notes by accident) beats exhaustive search; link density and randomized review surfaces buried gems.

## Apex practices
- Capture in a low-friction format (voice memo, quick text) but immediately refactor into structured notes; instant capture prevents loss of momentum.
- Use atomic notes (one idea per note) rather than lengthy files; atomicity enables recombination and linking.
- Build a "hub" or index: periodic synthesis of themes and connections. It's labor-intensive but surfaces patterns.
- Export/backup frequently; note-taking systems are fragile (vendor closures, sync failures).

## Pitfalls
- Perfectionism in note-taking (spending 2 hours formatting) at the expense of capture; capture first, beautify second.
- Accumulation without review; thousands of unreviewed notes provide no value.
- Closed-silo notes (inaccessible after the person leaves); future-proof systems prioritize portability.

## Tools & references
Obsidian (local, linked), Roam Research / Athens Research (networked), Zettelkasten method (Luhmann), spaced repetition (Anki).
