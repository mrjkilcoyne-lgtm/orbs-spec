# Video Scripting

## Scope
Writing for video: pacing, visual-narrative sync, tone, and structure optimized for viewing (not reading).

## Core principles
- Video is heard, not read; scripts for video are sparse, conversational, and use shorter sentences than written prose.
- Show-don't-tell is mandatory in video: narration describes what viewers are seeing (or are about to see). Narration that repeats visuals is redundant.
- Pacing is timing: scripts should leave room for visual B-roll, transitions, and let information land. Dense narration with no breathing room is exhausting.
- The first 3 seconds are do-or-die: hook with a question, surprise, or promise. Viewers can exit faster than they can read.
- Tone is voice and cadence; the script should signal pacing (shorter lines = faster), emotional beats (caps for emphasis, ellipses for pauses).

## Apex practices
- Structure scripts in two columns: visuals (left) and audio (right). This shows sync and makes editing/storyboarding easier.
- Time narration to match visuals: 150 words per minute is typical pace. Count words to estimate video length.
- Use natural language and contractions (you're, isn't) not formal prose (you are, is not).
- Include production notes for cameraman, editor: [CUT TO:], [PAUSE 2 SEC], [FADE TO:]. Be specific about intent.

## Pitfalls
- Over-writing; video scripts should be 30-50% shorter than equivalent prose to match pacing and viewing.
- Narration that talks heads into corners (nothing visual to show for 60 seconds); keep scenes short and varied.
- Forgetting that audio is half the production; a whispered confession requires different acoustics/mic technique than an upbeat explainer.

## Tools & references
Descript (transcript-first video editing), YouTube script templates, shot lists and storyboards, Final Cut Pro / DaVinci Resolve scripting features.
