# Compliance Frameworks

## Scope
The major security and privacy frameworks — SOC 2, ISO 27001, GDPR, and their neighbors — and how to satisfy them without letting compliance replace security.

## Core principles
- Know what each artifact proves: SOC 2 Type II is an auditor's opinion that your controls operated effectively over a period (6–12 months) against the Trust Services Criteria; Type I is a point-in-time design check; ISO 27001 certifies your ISMS (the management system that runs security) against Annex A controls; GDPR is law, not certification — fines up to 4% of global revenue, no certificate to hide behind.
- Controls follow from risk assessment, not from the checklist: both SOC 2 and ISO 27001 are scoped by what you determine is relevant (system description / Statement of Applicability) — a thoughtless scope produces an impressive report about the wrong things.
- GDPR's operational core: lawful basis for each processing purpose, data-subject rights with 30-day response clocks (access, erasure, portability), 72-hour breach notification to supervisory authorities, DPIAs for high-risk processing, and processor contracts (DPAs) flowing obligations downstream.
- Compliance is the floor and the forcing function, not the ceiling: frameworks encode a decade-old consensus baseline; real attackers are not bound by your auditor's sampling methodology — but the frameworks usefully force inventory, ownership, and evidence discipline that security benefits from.
- Evidence must be generated by systems, not people: point-in-time screenshots gathered in a pre-audit panic prove the panic; control evidence should be a byproduct of normal operations (tickets, pipeline logs, IdP reports) collected continuously.

## Apex practices
- Build one control set mapped to many frameworks (SOC 2 ↔ ISO 27001 Annex A ↔ NIST CSF crosswalks are well-established) so each new framework is a mapping exercise, not a new program.
- Automate evidence collection (Vanta/Drata-class tooling or your own pipelines from IdP, cloud config, MDM, ticketing) and monitor control drift continuously — failed-control alerts in week 3 beat audit findings in month 11.
- For GDPR, maintain the Article 30 record of processing activities and a real data map (what personal data, where, why, retention, processors); every privacy obligation hangs off knowing where the data is.
- Design controls you'd want anyway: access reviews that actually revoke, offboarding that actually disables, change management that's your normal PR flow — then the audit documents reality instead of a parallel theater.

## Pitfalls
- Writing policies that describe an imaginary company; auditors test what the policy says, so an aspirational policy converts overstatement into findings (or worse, into misrepresentation).
- Scoping the audit so narrowly it's meaningless — customers increasingly read the system description and bridge letters, and a SOC 2 covering one marketing site fools fewer buyers every year.
- Treating the annual audit as the security program: controls that "operate" for the evidence sample while the unmeasured 90% (real patching, real alerting) decays.

## Tools & references
