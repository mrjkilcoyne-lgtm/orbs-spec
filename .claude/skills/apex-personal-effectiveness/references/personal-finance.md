# Personal Finance

## Scope
Mapping income, expenses, and capital: budgeting, saving, investing, debt management, and alignment with long-term goals.

## Core principles
- The income-expense gap is the lever: you can't optimize your way out of spending more than you earn; the first order of business is deciding on a savings rate (20–50% of after-tax income) before any investment strategy.
- Compound interest is exponential, not just "good": at 7% real annual return, money doubles every 10 years; starting to invest at 25 vs. 35 is a 2x difference by 65, even if you save the same total (Bogle, Vanguard).
- Lifestyle inflation is the drag on wealth-building: each raise or windfall gets absorbed by expenses unless you explicitly allocate it to savings or investment; the discipline is boring and non-negotiable.
- Tax-advantaged accounts (401k, Roth IRA, 529) are not optional for high earners — the government is gifting you 30–40% returns via deductions and compound growth; not using them is leaving money on the table.
- Debt is leverage: high-interest debt (credit cards, auto loans) is a wealth drain; low-interest debt (mortgages, student loans at <4%) can be fine if you invest the difference at higher expected returns (arbitrage).

## Apex practices
- Track actual spending for two weeks with a simple app or spreadsheet to calibrate your mental model; most people overestimate what they spend on small items and underestimate fixed costs.
- Use a simple rule-based allocator: 50% needs, 30% wants, 20% savings — then automate all three (bills, discretionary envelope, automatic investment transfer) so you don't think about it.
- Max out tax-advantaged accounts before taxable investing: 401k contribution limits (2024: $23,500), Roth IRA ($7,000), HSA ($4,150 individual) are first priorities.
- Invest in low-cost, diversified index funds (US stock, international, bonds in proportions matching risk tolerance) rather than picking individual stocks or timing markets; Bogle's three-fund portfolio is the template.

## Pitfalls
- Trying to time the market or pick winning stocks based on research or hot tips — professional investors rarely outperform indexes after fees; individual investors almost never do.
- Carrying high-interest debt while investing in stock markets — a guaranteed 15% return (paying off credit card) beats an uncertain 7% market return.
- Conflating net worth with income or spending power — high net worth with illiquid assets or unaffordable lifestyle is fragile.

## Tools & references
Bogle's "The Little Book of Common Sense Investing," Vanguard/Fidelity low-cost index funds, YNAB (You Need A Budget) or Mint for tracking, IRS tax guides for retirement accounts.
