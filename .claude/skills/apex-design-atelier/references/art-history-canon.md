# Art History as a Toolbox

## Scope
The movement timeline — classical → Renaissance → Baroque → Romanticism → Impressionism → the modernisms → contemporary — held not as trivia but as a library of solved visual problems.

## Core principles
- Every movement is a reaction: Impressionism against academic finish, Cubism against single-point truth, Pop against abstract seriousness, Arts and Crafts against the machine. To use a movement, know what it was arguing with.
- Movements are constraint sets: Impressionism = broken color + captured light + visible hand; adopt the constraints and you inherit the look honestly instead of pastiching it.
- The canon rhymes across media: chiaroscuro became film noir lighting became dark-mode UI; the Renaissance grid became Swiss typography became CSS Grid.
- Form innovations outlive their subjects: nobody needs another water-lily pond, but broken color still solves "make light vibrate on a screen."
- Provenance gives authority: naming your borrowed system (De Stijl palette, Baroque diagonal) turns styling into argument.

## Apex practices
- Keep a personal lineage file per project: three works the design descends from, and what specifically was taken (palette, composition, attitude).
- Steal structure, not surface: take Vermeer's window-light logic, not his color-graded Instagram filter.
- Date-check your instincts: when a design feels "classic" or "futuristic," identify which decade it's actually quoting — control the reference instead of being controlled by it.
- Study transitions (Cézanne between Impressionism and Cubism) — the in-between artists show how visual systems mutate, which is the skill itself.

## Pitfalls
- Moodboard archaeology: quoting a movement's clichés (Warhol colorways, Mondrian rectangles) without its logic.
- Treating art history as European painting only — printmaking, textiles, calligraphy, and non-Western canons solved different problems worth stealing.
- Reverence: the canon is material, not scripture.

## Tools & references
Gombrich "The Story of Art," Janson, "Ways of Seeing" (Berger), museum open-access collections (Rijksmuseum, Met, NGA).
