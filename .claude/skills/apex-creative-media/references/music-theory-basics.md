# Music Theory Basics

## Scope
Foundational language of music: intervals, scales, chords, harmony, rhythm, and how to understand and construct melodies and chord progressions.

## Core principles
- Pitch is organized into octaves (doubling or halving frequency); within an octave, equal temperament divides it into 12 semitones, and scales select 7 (major) or 7 (natural minor) of those 12.
- Intervals (the distance between pitches) have names and emotional colors: perfect 5th is stable, major 3rd is bright, minor 3rd is dark, tritone is tension; these are physics (ratios of frequencies) and perception (cultural training).
- Chords (stacked thirds: root + 3rd + 5th + 7th) build harmony; major triads are stable, minor triads are introspective, 7th chords create tension, diminished are unstable; voice leading (how voices move between chords) makes progressions smooth.
- Rhythm is ratio-based: a half note is twice a quarter note; time signatures (4/4, 3/4, 6/8) organize beats into measures; syncopation (emphasizing off-beats) creates tension.
- Melody is the hierarchy of notes (some are more important than others) within a scale; a strong melody has a clear contour (rising, falling, shape) and resting tones (harmonic anchor points).

## Apex practices
- Learn intervals by ear and by sight: sing/play a major 3rd, minor 3rd, perfect 5th until they're automatic — ear training is a skill, not talent.
- Study progressions from songs you like: I-IV-V-I, ii-V-I (jazz standard), vi-IV-I-V (modern pop) are templates; understand why they work (chord function: tonic, subdominant, dominant).
- Sing melody over chords to understand how harmony supports melody; it's not theory, it's music.
- Experiment with constraint: compose a melody with only 3 notes, or a progression using only I and IV — limitations force creativity.

## Pitfalls
- Confusing the major scale with "happy" or the minor scale with "sad" — context, tempo, and arrangement matter far more.
- Assuming theory constrains creativity; it's the opposite — knowing the rules makes breaking them effective.
- Memorizing chord names without understanding how to use them.

## Tools & references
