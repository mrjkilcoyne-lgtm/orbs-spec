# Positioning

## Scope
Defining where the product sits in the market: who it's for, what problem it solves, and why it's different from alternatives.

## Core principles
- Positioning is not tagline; it's the psychological "slot" in a buyer's mind. Buyers use positioning to answer "is this for me or not?" in seconds — if it's unclear, they disqualify themselves.
- Positioning starts with the beachhead (narrowest wedge segment where the problem is severe, the buyer has budget, and you can win decisively); expanding positioning often fails because it dilutes the core message.
- Alternatives include direct competitors (Slack vs. Teams), indirect competitors (email for team comms), and do-nothing (the default). Most customers compare against status quo, not your competitor.
- Positioning must resonate with the buyer's worldview; telling an engineer "it's easy" is noise if they care about "it's powerful." Understand the buyer persona's values first.
- Effective positioning isolates one dimension that matters most (fastest, cheapest, easiest, most private) and owns it; trying to win on all dimensions looks generic.

## Apex practices
- Build a position statement (for [buyer], [product] is the [category] that [benefit] unlike [alternative] which [alternative's limitation]).
- Test positioning with buyer interviews (5-10 target customers, "what problem is this solving and why choose it?"); if their answer differs from your position, iterate.
- Align sales messaging, product marketing, and customer success on the same positioning; misalignment kills trust.
- Revisit positioning quarterly; as competitors move or market conditions shift, your position may need adjustment.

## Pitfalls
- Positioning that is true but not convincing ("we have great UX" — nobody buys on this alone).
- Avoiding competitive positioning out of politeness; explicit comparison to the alternative (what the buyer will do instead) clarifies why to choose you.
- Changing positioning frequently because you're chasing shiny opportunities; consistency compounds; messaging consistency over a year drives more adoption than pivoting quarterly.
