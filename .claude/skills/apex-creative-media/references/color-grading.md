# Color Grading

## Scope
Systematic color manipulation of footage: LUTs, curves, hue/saturation, and establishing a cohesive emotional and visual tone across scenes.

## Core principles
- Color is information: saturation communicates certainty (desaturated feels cold/sad, saturated feels warm/confident), temperature (cool blues vs. warm oranges) communicates mood, and hue shifts the emotional read.
- Grading happens in layers: fix obvious color casts first (white balance), then establish tonal range (lift blacks, push whites), then stylize (color grades, look LUTs), then refine (local adjustments).
- A look is reproducible and scannable: if viewers consciously think about color, the grading is too strong (invisible grading serves the story); consistency across scenes signals professional work.
- Scopes (waveform, vectorscope, histogram) show you what you can't see on a monitor; they're the ground truth for exposure and color balance.
- Grading should not fix camera or lighting failures alone; good grading enhances good photography, it doesn't save bad ones.

## Apex practices
- Create a look early (first 2–3 clips) and apply it as a base to all subsequent clips; it's faster than grading each scene independently and ensures consistency.
- Use LUTs (Look-Up Tables) as starting points, then adjust locally: a LUT gives you direction, but every scene needs tweaking based on exposure and lighting variations.
- Grade on calibrated monitors (Rec.709 or DCI-P3, depending on deliverable) in a controlled room (standard lighting, contrast ratios, viewing angle).
- Use HSL wheels (color wheels per tonal range: shadows, midtones, highlights) to push color in specific frequency bands; it's more sophisticated than global HSL.

## Pitfalls
- Grading on an uncalibrated monitor and having the look shift when viewed elsewhere (phone, TV, cinema).
- Over-grading (crushing blacks, clipping colors, extreme saturation) — restraint reads as professional.
- Ignoring exposure correction before color grading; fixing exposure first saves time and improves results.

## Tools & references
DaVinci Resolve (de facto grading standard, free tier), Speedgrade (Adobe deprecated, legacy), Premiere Pro's Lumetri, Rec.709 and DCI-P3 standards, LUT libraries (Look LUTs, Mettle), colorist studies.
