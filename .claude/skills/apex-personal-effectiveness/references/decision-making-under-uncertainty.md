# Decision-Making Under Uncertainty

## Scope
Choosing well when outcomes are probabilistic: expected value, decision quality vs. outcome quality, and processes that resist bias.

## Core principles
- Judge decisions by process, not outcome ("resulting," Duke): a good bet can lose and a bad bet can win; grading by outcome teaches superstition — grade by whether the probabilities and payoffs were assessed well with the information available.
- Expected value is the baseline but not the whole story: EV maximization is right for repeated, affordable bets; for ruin-risk bets, Kelly-style thinking applies — never risk what you can't afford to lose for what you don't need (ergodicity matters).
- Reversibility determines speed (Bezos's Type 1/Type 2 doors): irreversible, consequential decisions deserve slow deliberation; reversible ones should be made fast with ~70% of the information you wish you had — waiting for 90% is the expensive error.
- Probabilistic beliefs beat binary ones: forecasters who think in granular probabilities and update incrementally (Tetlock's superforecasters) outperform experts who think in yes/no — small frequent updates, no dramatic flips.
- Under deep uncertainty, optionality beats optimization: when you can't estimate probabilities, prefer choices that keep more future paths open and cap downside (Taleb's barbell) over the highest-point-estimate path.

## Apex practices
- Run a premortem (Klein) before committing: "it's a year later and this decision failed — write the story"; prospective hindsight surfaces risks brainstorming misses.
- Write a decision journal: the decision, the options considered, the probabilities assigned, what would change your mind — then review quarterly; memory rewrites history, the journal doesn't.
- Set tripwires in advance ("if churn exceeds X by March, we kill it") — pre-committed exit criteria beat in-the-moment judgment corrupted by sunk costs (Heath's WRAP framework).
- Widen the frame: "whether or not" decisions have a base failure rate near 50% (Nutt's research); generating even one extra genuine alternative measurably improves outcomes.

## Pitfalls
- Sunk-cost escalation: continuing because of what's already invested — the invested amount is identical in both futures and therefore irrelevant to the choice.
- Analysis paralysis on two-way doors: burning weeks to de-risk a decision that costs one day to reverse.
- Point-estimate planning: building on the single "most likely" scenario instead of ranges — the plan that survives only the median case fails in most actual futures.

## Tools & references
Duke's "Thinking in Bets," Heath & Heath's "Decisive" (WRAP), Tetlock's "Superforecasting," Klein's premortem (HBR 2007), Kelly criterion, decision journals.
