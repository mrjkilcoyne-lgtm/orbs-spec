# Creative Direction

## Scope
Running the studio: taste, critique, iteration cadence, and holding many hands to one vision — the layer above craft that makes bodies of work coherent.

## Core principles
- Direction is decision compression: a creative director's job is converting infinite possibility into a small set of committed choices (the brief, the references, the no-list) so that many makers converge without homogenizing. Vision that can't be stated as constraints is mood, not direction.
- Taste is trained, not innate: it's a corpus of internalized comparisons — you can only recognize "this ending is weak" if you've metabolized a thousand endings. Deliberate input diet (the canon, competitors, adjacent fields) is a professional obligation, and the taste-skill gap (Ira Glass) closes only through shipped volume.
- Critique targets the work's intent, not the critic's preference: first establish what the piece is trying to do, then judge whether it does it, then — separately, and only if asked — whether it's the right thing to try. The three questions must not blur.
- Coherence beats brilliance in bodies of work: ten pieces at 8/10 sharing one spine outperform ten disconnected 9s — campaigns, product suites, and portfolios are judged as systems (the Mondrian lesson at organizational scale).
- Protect the weird early: first-round ideas are fragile and committees sand them round; the director's role is to shelter the distinctive element through the drafts where it looks like a mistake, and kill it only by deliberate decision.

## Apex practices
- Direct with references and anti-references: three works that share the target's spirit, three that look adjacent but are wrong, each with one sentence on why — the fastest vision-transfer format ever devised.
- Run critique with a protocol: maker states intent (2 min) → observers describe what they see before judging → strengths → questions → suggestions marked as optional; and never let the room redesign live.
- Set an iteration cadence with divergence and convergence phases marked: many cheap variants early (no polishing), ruthless selection at the gate, then depth on the survivor — and communicate which phase you're in, since feedback rules differ per phase.
- Keep a kill log: ideas cut, with why — half of direction is the no-list, and the log turns painful cuts into reusable doctrine.

## Pitfalls
- Directing by doing: the director who repaints every canvas produces one tired artist and a team of stenographers.
- Taste tyranny: "I'll know it when I see it" — unstateable standards burn infinite iterations and team morale.
- Polishing in the divergence phase and brainstorming in the convergence phase — cadence inversion, the most common studio process failure.
