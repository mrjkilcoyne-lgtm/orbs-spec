# Reading Research Papers

## Scope
Efficiently extracting value from published research: identifying key claims, evaluating evidence quality, and integrating findings into mental models.

## Core principles
- Abstracts lie by omission: they highlight novel contributions but bury limitations, sample sizes, and effect sizes. Read the full paper, not just the abstract.
- Methods and results are more important than discussion; claims in the discussion are speculative. Check whether methods align with claims (large claims + small sample = weak evidence).
- Effect sizes and confidence intervals matter more than p-values; a highly significant but tiny effect (p<0.001, Cohen's d=0.1) is noise in practice.
- Look for conflicts of interest (funding, author affiliations); results strongly favoring a funder or company warrant extra skepticism.
- Supplementary materials and code availability enable checking; if not provided, the paper is less reproducible (higher bar for believing it).

## Core principles (continued)
- Replication and meta-analysis trump single studies; a result cited 100 times is more credible than one with n=30 and no replications.
- Citations are not endorsements; citing a paper means the authors read it, not agreed with it. Read the citing papers to understand the debate.

## Apex practices
- Skim first pass: title, abstract, figures, main results section. Understand the question and main finding without drowning in details.
- Read methods carefully on second pass; every deviation from gold-standard procedure (blinding, randomization, power analysis) is a red flag.
- Look for the funding statement, conflict-of-interest declaration, and author affiliations; they provide context.
- Check if the paper publishes raw data, materials, and code; if yes, it's higher confidence; if no, reproducibility is lower.
- Use backward citation search (references cited by this paper) and forward search (papers citing this one); both reveal the conversation and controversies.

## Pitfalls
- Believing the conclusions without reading methods; a paper concluding "X causes Y" without randomization is claiming correlation as causation.
- Over-weighting recent papers; older papers can have been replicated many times and are often more reliable.
- Assuming all papers in peer-reviewed journals are high-quality; peer review is a bar, not a guarantee. Predatory journals and low-quality reviews exist.

## Tools & references
Keshav's "How to Read a Paper," PubMed, arXiv (preprints), Retraction Watch (tracking errors/fraud), Cochrane methodology for evidence synthesis.
