# Accounting Fundamentals

## Scope
The bedrock of financial reporting: double-entry bookkeeping, chart of accounts, accrual vs. cash basis, and the mechanics of how transactions flow into financial statements.

## Core principles
- Double-entry bookkeeping (every transaction has two sides: a debit and a credit) is not optional ceremony — it's the constraint that forces consistency; if your books don't balance, you have a fraud or an error, full stop.
- Accrual accounting (record revenue when earned, expenses when incurred, not when cash moves) matches economics to the period; cash accounting hides the truth (you could have deferred revenue or unpaid bills that cash basis sweeps under the rug).
- The chart of accounts is the skeleton of your financial system: asset/liability/equity/revenue/expense accounts organized in a hierarchy; a broken COA (mixing operational and non-operational expenses, failing to sub-account by department) makes reporting impossible.
- Materiality is a threshold: a $50 office supply is expensed; a $5,000 stapler is capitalized and depreciated. The auditor and tax authority want consistency; pick a policy (usually $1-2k for mid-size companies) and stick to it.
- Reconciliation (matching ledger balances to bank statements, supplier invoices, GL accounts) is the heartbeat of accounting control; companies that don't reconcile monthly are one undetected error away from being insolvent without knowing it.

## Apex practices
- Implement a 30/60/90-day close calendar and stick to it; predictability lets controllers train staff and gives finance predictable input to decisions.
- Use a cloud-based GL (QuickBooks, NetSuite, Adaptive Insights) that auto-syncs to bank feeds and supplier portals; manual entry is where 90% of errors hide.
- Separate your GL accounts for revenue into buckets by type (product, service, interest income) and geography so you can see where money comes from without re-keying in Excel.
- Audit your revenue recognition policy once a year with your auditor; one revenue rule slip (booking Q5 revenue in Q4) propagates through the year and kills comparability.

## Pitfalls
- Recording expenses by calendar month instead of matching them to the period they benefit (Jan office rent accrued in Jan); accrual entries require discipline or revenue/expense will drift.
- Using the same expense account for everything operational; managers and auditors can't see patterns in spend without detailed sub-accounts.
- Delaying reconciliation because "we'll do it in the close"; unreconciled months compound errors and kill audit confidence.

## Tools & references
GAAP (Generally Accepted Accounting Principles) for US companies, IFRS for international, Investopedia's "Accounting Basics," nonprofit accounting (modified accrual), IMA (Institute of Management Accountants) standards.
