# Memo Writing

## Scope
Internal communication for alignment: conciseness, clarity of action, and appropriate formality.

## Core principles
- Memos are for coordination, not documentation; they're written to inform a specific group about a decision or change. Email or Slack after it lands.
- The memo structure (TO/FROM/DATE, subject, summary, details) has persisted because it works: subject line should make the gist clear in 5 words.
- Audience sensitivity: a memo to executives differs from one to individual contributors in abstraction level and emphasis. Know who's reading and why.
- Memos should have a clear call-to-action: what does the reader need to do? Approve? Acknowledge? Adjust behavior? Say it explicitly.
- Brevity is respect for the reader's time. A one-page memo beats a five-page essay even if the essay is better-written.

## Apex practices
- Use BLUF (Bottom Line Up Front): lead with the recommendation or summary, then details. Readers can stop after the summary if they trust you.
- Include context briefly (why now, why this) but don't belabor it; assume the reader is informed.
- Use bullets for action items, timelines, or multi-part reasoning; paragraphs for narrative.
- Address objections or alternatives preemptively; shows you've thought deeply.

## Pitfalls
- Burying decisions under context; decisions should be prominent and unambiguous.
- Tone-deafness: a casual memo on a serious issue (layoffs, strategy shift) erodes trust. Match gravity to topic.
- Overexplaining: "We're moving offices on March 1. New address: ..." is enough; don't narrate the decision-making process.

## Tools & references
Pyramid Principle (memos as miniature strategic communication), internal communication audit, memo templates (AWS Shareholder Letters).
