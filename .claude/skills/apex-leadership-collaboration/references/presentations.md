# Presentations

## Scope
Designing and delivering talks and slide decks — technical talks, exec updates, and all-hands presentations.

## Core principles
- One talk, one message: the audience will remember a single sentence a week later, so decide what that sentence is before opening the slide tool — everything that doesn't serve it is decoration or damage.
- Audience-first design: an exec review, a conference talk, and a team deep-dive on the same project are three different talks; the deck built for all three serves none (know their question: "should I fund this?" vs "how does it work?").
- Slides are visual aids, not documents or teleprompters: if a slide works when read without you, it's a memo; if you read it aloud, the audience reads faster than you speak and stops listening (redundancy effect, Mayer's multimedia research).
- Structure carries weak delivery, but delivery cannot carry weak structure: SCQA (Situation, Complication, Question, Answer) or problem→stakes→resolution gives any material a spine.
- Nerves are physiological and front-loaded: the first 90 seconds are the worst, so script and over-rehearse only the opening — momentum carries the rest.

## Apex practices
- Build the talk outside the slide tool: sticky notes or an outline first, slides last — slideware-first design produces bullet lists shaped by the software's defaults, not the argument.
- Rehearse aloud, standing, timed, at least three times; the third run is where phrasing stops surprising you (and it always runs 20% longer live than at your desk).
- For exec audiences: answer-first, then evidence; put the ask on slide one and be ready to be cut off at any slide with the message intact (the "commander's brief" discipline).
- Design one takeaway visual per section — a chart or diagram that makes the point without narration — because visuals are what survives the memory bottleneck.

## Pitfalls
- The wall of bullets read verbatim: the audience finishes the slide in 8 seconds, you finish in 90, and the gap is filled with phones.
- Burying the ask: 25 minutes of context before "we need two engineers" — by then the room is checked out and the decision deferred.
- Ending with a limp Q&A instead of a close; take questions second-to-last, then land your final message so the room leaves on your sentence, not a tangent.
