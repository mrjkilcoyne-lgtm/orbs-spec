# Valuation

## Scope
Determining company value using multiple approaches (DCF, comparables, precedent transactions, venture method) and understanding how investors price risk.

## Core principles
- The only "correct" valuation is the price at which two parties agree; all methods are just frameworks for that negotiation. A DCF might suggest $20M but a buyer only wants $10M — the truth is $10M.
- Venture valuations are priced on risk, not on current cash flow (which is often negative); early-stage value is entirely option value on future exits — the business plan, team quality, and market size drive valuation.
- Discounted cash flow (DCF) requires three inputs: cash flow projections (most sensitive), terminal growth rate (often ignored, it comprises 60-80% of value), and discount rate (cost of capital adjusted for risk).
- Venture method (work backward from exit value) is the clearest for startups: if the fund needs 10x returns in 7 years, and you expect $100M acquisition, then Series A valuation = $100M / 10 ÷ (1 + discount rate)^7, which forces alignment between valuation and expectations.
- Comparable company multiples (revenue, EBITDA, subscriber multiples) are useful for matured SaaS but dangerous for early stage (Slack was "unprofitable" by tech standards pre-IPO; Airbnb had negative unit economics early).

## Apex practices
- Build a scenario-based DCF (bull, base, bear cases) with 3-year detailed projections and terminal value assumptions; sensitivity analysis (what if discount rate is 15% vs. 20%?) shows which assumptions matter.
- For private companies, check precedent transactions (what did similar companies sell for?) as a reality-check on multiples; a $10M revenue SaaS company should be 5-8x revenue, not 20x.
- Use the venture method to sanity-check your fundraising expectations; if you're raising at $50M post-money and the fund expects $500M exits, you're calibrated; if they expect $50M exits, that's a low-confidence investment.
- Remember that valuation and valuation per share are different; a 20% valuation increase and 50% option pool increase can leave each shareholder holding 60% of 1x value (the shares are worth more, but dilution offsets).

## Pitfalls
- Anchoring on a valuation from one investor as the "market price"; different investors price risk differently based on their check size and return requirements.
- Using revenue multiples from public SaaS companies for private companies; public multiples price in profitability and scale (Salesforce at 8x revenue), while private SaaS often trades at 2-3x revenue.
- Ignoring dilution in valuation math; a company raising at $100M post-money with a 50% option pool is cheaper than it appears per share.
