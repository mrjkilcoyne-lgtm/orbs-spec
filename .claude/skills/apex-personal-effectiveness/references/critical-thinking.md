# Critical Thinking

## Scope
Evaluating claims, arguments, and evidence: logic, cognitive biases, source assessment, and structured skepticism.

## Core principles
- Confirmation bias is the master bias: we seek, weight, and remember evidence that fits (Wason's 2-4-6 task); the corrective is deliberately generating and testing the strongest disconfirming case, not "being open-minded."
- Arguments have structure: distinguish validity (conclusion follows from premises) from soundness (premises are also true) — most bad reasoning smuggles in a false or unstated premise rather than a formal fallacy.
- Base rates dominate vivid evidence (Bayes): a 99%-accurate test for a 1-in-10,000 condition still yields mostly false positives; always ask "what's the prior?" before updating on a dramatic data point.
- Strength of evidence is a hierarchy: anecdote < expert opinion < observational study < RCT < systematic review/meta-analysis — and effect size plus replication status matter more than any single p-value (the replication crisis is the cautionary tale).
- Motivated reasoning scales with intelligence: smarter people are better at rationalizing what they already believe (Kahan's studies), so procedure — pre-commitment, steelmanning, calibration — beats raw IQ.

## Apex practices
- Steelman before rebutting: state the opposing view so well its holders would endorse it (Rapoport's rules via Dennett); if you can't, you don't yet understand the disagreement.
- Assign explicit probabilities to claims and track calibration over time — "probably" hides between 51% and 95%; forecasting practice (Tetlock) is the gym for this.
- Trace claims to primary sources: press releases and abstracts routinely overstate papers; check sample size, control group, and whether the measured outcome is the claimed outcome.
- Run the "consider the opposite" drill on your own conclusions (Lord, Lepper & Preston, 1984) — the single intervention with the best evidence for debiasing judgment.

## Pitfalls
- Fallacy-spotting as a substitute for engagement — naming "ad hominem!" while ignoring that the argument may still be sound.
- Symmetric cynicism ("all sources lie") — indiscriminate distrust is as lazy as indiscriminate trust; calibrated trust in track records is the skill.
- Mistaking contrarianism for independence: rejecting the consensus has the same epistemic laziness as accepting it, without the base-rate advantage.

## Tools & references
Kahneman's "Thinking, Fast and Slow," Tetlock's "Superforecasting," Sagan's baloney-detection kit, Dennett's "Intuition Pumps" (Rapoport's rules), calibration tools like Metaculus/GJ Open.
